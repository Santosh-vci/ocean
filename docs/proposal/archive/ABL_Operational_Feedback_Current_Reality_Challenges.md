# ABL Transshipment Operations  
## Current Reality and Business-Side Feedback Synthesis

**Document purpose:**  
This document converts the first business interaction feedback into a generic operations-side summary. It removes individual speaker references and captures the current reality, constraints, operational pain points, and implied improvement direction for ABL’s transshipment scheduling and fleet management environment.

---

## 1. Executive Summary

ABL is operating in a highly dynamic coal transshipment environment where demand, jetty allocation, coal quality mix, OGV schedules, tug-barge availability, floating crane availability, river conditions, tide windows, bridge constraints, and field execution decisions must all remain synchronized.

The current operating model is largely reactive. The client-side production and coal quality plan changes frequently, while the logistics fleet must adjust quickly to keep the OGV loading process moving. This creates repeated reassignment, waiting, idle time, low asset utilization, and preventable delays such as low-tide grounding.

The core challenge is not merely the absence of scheduling software. The deeper issue is that a dynamic, end-to-end logistics flow is being managed through static planning, fragmented visibility, manual judgment, and inconsistent operational reporting. The required solution should therefore be positioned as a live scheduling, simulation, and decision-support system backed by a common operating process.

---

## 2. Current Operating Context

ABL supports coal movement from multiple jetties to OGVs through tug-and-barge operations, floating cranes / CTS assets, and related transshipment infrastructure.

The overall flow includes:

1. Coal production and stock availability at different jetties.
2. Buyer-specific coal quality and blending requirements.
3. OGV arrival and loading schedule.
4. Assignment of tug-barge sets to jetties and OGVs.
5. River movement subject to tide, bridge, traffic, weather, and safety constraints.
6. Loading at jetty, sailing, unloading/transshipment, and return/reassignment cycle.

The operation depends heavily on synchronized timing. If one node changes, the entire downstream flow is affected.

---

## 3. Demand and Planning Instability

The demand signal is generated by the coal producer/client, based on the monthly cargo plan, OGV requirements, buyer commitments, coal quality, and expected production from specific jetties.

However, the production and loading plan is revised frequently:

- A monthly plan may assume production from a balanced set of jetties.
- Within a week, production may shift from nearby jetties to faraway jetties.
- Coal quality requirements may change the required jetty combination.
- OGV or buyer delays may require reassignment of already-planned trips.
- A tug-barge set may be sent toward one jetty or OGV and later be redirected.

This causes fleet demand to fluctuate. A plan that requires 10 tug-barge sets in one week may suddenly require more because cargo is now coming from a faraway jetty. Later, the requirement may reduce again, creating idle time or underutilization.

### Business impact

The logistics team must absorb demand variability without having full control over the upstream production and quality decisions. This creates a persistent mismatch between planned fleet capacity and actual operational requirement.

---

## 4. Jetty Distance and Cycle-Time Variability

The operating network has multiple jetties with materially different travel times.

Indicative operating reality:

- Nearest jetty movement may involve roughly 9 hours of sailing plus loading/unloading time.
- The farthest jetty may take 48–70 hours, practically close to a three-day cycle.
- Return movement may be faster when the barge is empty, but the return destination may change depending on the next instruction.

This means that the same number of fleet assets cannot be treated as a fixed capacity pool. A tug-barge set serving a nearby jetty can rotate much faster than one assigned to a distant jetty.

### Business impact

When production shifts from nearer jetties to farther jetties, fleet requirement increases sharply. If this change is not anticipated early enough, the operation either suffers delays or needs urgent chartering of additional vessels.

---

## 5. Coal Quality and Blending Complexity

The operation is not a simple volume movement problem. It is also a coal quality and blending problem.

Each OGV / buyer may require a specific coal quality. The same final quality may be achieved through different combinations of coal from different jetties. Therefore, one OGV may receive cargo from two or three jetties depending on availability and blending logic.

Indicative operating reality:

- One OGV may carry around 60,000–70,000 MT.
- One OGV may require around 7 tug-barge loads depending on barge size and cargo quantity.
- Some of these tug-barge loads may be reassigned from other OGVs or buyer plans.
- A tug-barge set already aligned to one buyer/quality combination may not be easily swapped because the quality requirement is specific.

### Business impact

Quality-locking reduces reassignment flexibility. Once a tug-barge load is linked to a specific quality/buyer/OGV combination, it cannot always be redirected without recalculating the blending plan. This creates delay when the OGV or buyer plan changes.

---

## 6. Static Planning in a Dynamic Environment

The current planning cycle can be described as:

**Monthly plan → weekly revision → daily revision → real-time field changes**

The issue is that the plan keeps changing, but the scheduling logic and field decision process do not move with the same speed.

Dynamic variables include:

- OGV ETA / delay
- Buyer requirement
- Coal quality combination
- Jetty readiness
- Stockpile availability
- Tug-barge availability
- Floating crane availability
- Tide window
- Bridge opening window
- Weather
- Breakdown / maintenance
- Field congestion and traffic

### Business impact

A static plan is unable to remain valid under continuously changing field conditions. The operation needs live recalculation and simulation rather than only periodic rescheduling.

---

## 7. Fleet Productivity and Idle Time

Current productivity appears constrained by waiting and uncertainty.

Indicative feedback from the discussion:

- Tug-and-barge productive time may be around 55–60%.
- Unproductive time may be around 40–45%.
- Floating crane productive time may be lower, around 35%.

Unproductive time includes:

- Waiting for jetty availability
- Waiting for floating crane availability
- Queuing
- Waiting for next instruction
- Waiting due to OGV delay
- Waiting due to tide or bridge constraints
- Idle time due to changed production or quality plans

### Business impact

ABL has significant opportunity to improve asset utilization by reducing avoidable waiting, improving pre-assignment logic, and creating a better decision process for when to release, hold, or redirect assets.

---

## 8. Tug-Barge Scheduling Gaps

The floating crane process is comparatively simpler because the crane largely waits near the mother vessel / transshipment point and loads the OGV. The tug-and-barge process is more complex because each trip must manage origin, destination, sailing time, tide, bridge, jetty queue, loading, unloading, return path, and reassignment.

Current gaps include:

- No certain trip cycle for all tug-barge movements.
- Tug masters may start movement without confirmed final assignment.
- Return destination may be known initially but can change midway.
- Tide-aware and bridge-aware dispatching is not system-driven.
- Field decisions depend heavily on experience and local judgment.

### Business impact

The highest complexity lies in tug-and-barge flow control. A scheduling solution must focus strongly on tug-barge release timing, route feasibility, reassignment logic, and safe waiting strategy.

---

## 9. Tide, Bridge, and River Navigation Constraints

River movement is constrained by natural and operational windows.

Key constraints include:

- Low tide can cause grounding.
- High tide can be used strategically to move more smoothly.
- Bridge windows can create stoppage if not aligned with vessel movement.
- Sailing speed may need to be adjusted to match tide and river conditions.
- Traffic and congestion may further affect actual passage time.

The field reality is that some experienced masters may wait at a suitable point when they anticipate a tide issue, while others may continue and become stuck.

### Business impact

Tide and bridge data may be knowable, but it is not consistently converted into actionable dispatch guidance. This causes avoidable stoppage and grounding.

---

## 10. Grounding as a Major Constraint

The most critical operational constraint identified is grounding due to low tide.

This should not be treated simply as a field execution mistake. Grounding often occurs because the system pushes assets to keep moving in order to avoid visible idle time. However, premature movement can lead to worse delay than controlled waiting.

### Important operating insight

Sometimes waiting is the correct operational decision.

A tug-barge set should not always be released just because it is available. If it is likely to hit a low-tide bottleneck, it may be better to hold it at a safe location, release it later, and complete the movement faster with less risk.

### Business impact

The organization needs a way to distinguish bad idle time from intelligent waiting. Without that distinction, field teams are pressured to move assets even when waiting would reduce total lead time.

---

## 11. Jetty Waiting and Loading Time

At the jetty, actual loading time may be around 3–4 hours, but average waiting before loading may be much higher, potentially around 12 hours depending on conditions.

A practical cycle from unloading at the transshipment point to getting loaded again can become approximately 24 hours, including:

1. Waiting for next instruction or confirmation.
2. Sailing to the next jetty.
3. Waiting / queuing at the jetty.
4. Loading at the jetty.

### Business impact

Reducing this post-discharge-to-next-load cycle time can directly improve fleet rotation and asset utilization.

---

## 12. Limited Live Operational Visibility

The operation has some visibility through AIS or position tracking, but position alone is not enough.

Current limitations include:

- Asset position may be known.
- Actual activity status may not be known reliably.
- Tug masters may not consistently submit operational reports.
- Floating crane reporting may follow a different standard from tug-barge reporting.
- Operational databases exist, but the data may not be fully reliable or timely.

### Business impact

The business may know where an asset is, but not necessarily whether it is sailing, waiting, loading, unloading, delayed, grounded, under maintenance, or available for reassignment. This weakens both real-time scheduling and post-facto performance analysis.

---

## 13. Charter Fleet Lead Time

ABL uses a mix of owned and chartered tugboats.

Indicative feedback:

- ABL owns a smaller base fleet.
- Additional fleet is chartered depending on demand fluctuation.
- Preparing a new third-party tugboat for operation may take around one week due to inspection and readiness checks.

### Business impact

Fleet capacity cannot be added instantly. If the upstream production plan changes late, ABL may not have enough time to safely add external capacity. Therefore, early visibility and rolling capacity forecast are essential.

---

## 14. Field Decision-Making and Accountability Gap

A major current reality is that field teams may hesitate to make non-obvious decisions such as holding a tug-barge set, delaying release, or redirecting an asset.

Reasons include:

- Fear of being blamed for visible idle time.
- Concern that waiting may be seen as laziness or underperformance.
- Different teams have different KPIs.
- No system-generated explanation exists to justify decisions.
- Field superintendents and masters may not want to take accountability for a decision that contradicts the usual “keep moving” mindset.

### Business impact

Even when a better operational decision is available, it may not be taken unless it is backed by a system, an agreed process, and top-level alignment.

---

## 15. KPI Misalignment Across Entities

The operation involves different organizational entities and teams. Each may optimize its own KPI:

- Jetty team may want to keep loading activity visible.
- Logistics team may want to improve fleet utilization.
- OGV / buyer side may want delivery on time.
- Field teams may want to avoid being blamed for waiting.

This creates local optimization. For example, loading a tug-barge immediately may make the jetty look productive, but the tug-barge may later wait at a bridge or become grounded during low tide.

### Business impact

The operation needs common KPIs that look at total flow performance, not only local departmental activity.

Recommended common objectives:

- Improve OGV loading reliability.
- Reduce demurrage / vessel delay risk.
- Improve tug-barge rotation.
- Reduce preventable waiting and grounding.
- Improve end-to-end lead time.
- Maintain safety and compliance.

---

## 16. Why a Simulation Layer Is Needed

The business requirement is better described as a simulation and decision-support problem rather than a simple scheduling problem.

A simulation layer should answer questions such as:

- Should this tug-barge be released now or held?
- If released now, will it hit a low-tide grounding window?
- Should it go to the far jetty or complete a nearer cycle first?
- What happens if the OGV is delayed by one day?
- Can a planned tug-barge be reassigned without violating quality requirements?
- What is the best use of available assets under current constraints?
- Which asset will become the next bottleneck?

### Business impact

A simulation-backed decision gives the field and office teams a rational basis for action. It also reduces subjective debate and blame.

---

## 17. Desired Future Operating Model

The desired operating model should move from reactive dispatching to synchronized, live flow control.

The future model should include:

1. Shared demand and OGV schedule visibility.
2. Jetty-wise production and coal quality availability.
3. Stockpile and cargo quality visibility.
4. Tug-barge live position and activity status.
5. Floating crane / CTS status.
6. Tide, bridge, weather, traffic, and safety constraints.
7. Dynamic scheduling and reassignment logic.
8. Simulation-based recommendations.
9. Field-level decision support.
10. Common dashboard for ABL and client-side stakeholders.

---

## 18. Required Decision Recommendations

The system should not only display data. It should actively recommend operational decisions.

Examples:

- Release tug-barge now.
- Hold tug-barge for tide window.
- Redirect tug-barge to alternate jetty.
- Keep current assignment because quality-locking prevents reassignment.
- Prioritize nearer cycle before far-jetty dispatch due to bridge closure.
- Recalculate OGV loading sequence due to delay.
- Trigger charter capacity warning due to projected fleet shortage.
- Alert that a planned movement will create grounding risk.

Each recommendation should show the reason behind the decision.

## 20. Key Improvement Opportunities

### 20.1 Reduce avoidable waiting
Identify and reduce waiting caused by unclear next assignment, late reassignment, jetty queue, floating crane mismatch, and avoidable release into known bottlenecks.

### 20.2 Improve release timing
Do not release tug-barge sets merely because they are available. Release them when the route, tide, bridge, jetty, and OGV condition make the movement feasible.

### 20.3 Create intelligent waiting buffers
Waiting is not always waste. The system should define where and when waiting is strategically better than moving.

### 20.4 Improve reassignment logic
When OGV, buyer, or jetty plans change, the system should evaluate feasible reassignment options while respecting coal quality and blending constraints.

### 20.5 Improve fleet capacity forecasting
ABL needs early visibility of future capacity gaps because chartering additional tugboats may take around one week.

### 20.6 Improve reporting reliability
AIS position must be complemented by activity status reporting. Without reliable activity status, real-time scheduling will remain weak.

### 20.7 Establish common KPIs
The operating model must shift from “my KPI vs your KPI” to shared flow KPIs across ABL and the client-side stakeholders.

---

## 21. Proposed Solution Direction

The proposed solution should be framed as an **Integrated Transshipment Scheduling and Simulation System**.

It should include three layers:

### Layer 1: Visibility Layer
A common operating dashboard showing demand, OGVs, jetties, tug-barge fleet, floating cranes, tide, bridge, weather, and live status.

### Layer 2: Scheduling Layer
Dynamic scheduling engine that assigns tug-barge sets to jetties and OGVs based on demand, quality, route, capacity, and availability.

### Layer 3: Simulation and Decision Layer
Scenario engine that evaluates operational decisions before execution and explains the recommended action.

---

## 22. Process Change Requirements

Software alone will not solve the problem unless the operating process changes.

Required process changes include:

1. Agree on a common decision process between ABL and client-side planning/marine teams.
2. Define which decisions are system-recommended and who approves exceptions.
3. Define when waiting is acceptable and how it is classified.
4. Define standard reason codes for delay, waiting, reassignment, and grounding.
5. Align KPIs around end-to-end flow and customer delivery.
6. Train field teams to follow system-backed release/hold/redirection recommendations.
7. Improve discipline of operational status reporting.

---

## 23. Conclusion

The current reality is a dynamic transshipment operation being managed without sufficient live synchronization. Production shifts, quality requirements, OGV changes, jetty variability, tide constraints, bridge windows, and fleet availability create a continuously changing planning environment.

The main opportunity is to move from static scheduling and reactive dispatching to a synchronized, simulation-backed operating model. The future system must help the business decide not only where each tug-barge should go, but also when it should move, when it should wait, and why that decision is operationally better.

The success of the solution will depend on both technology and process alignment. A shared dashboard, reliable live data, decision simulation, and common KPIs can help ABL and its client-side stakeholders reduce waiting, prevent grounding, improve fleet utilization, and protect OGV delivery commitments.
